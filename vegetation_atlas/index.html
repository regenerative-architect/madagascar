🇫🇷 Résumé en français · mode d’emploi local (traduction partielle)

Objectif : choisir les bonnes plantes selon le milieu naturel et améliorer les récoltes sans endommager la biodiversité de Madagascar. La version complète des fiches reste en anglais ; ce résumé ne remplace pas un conseil agronomique ou écologique local.

  1. Identifier le site : forêt humide, forêt sèche, fourré épineux, savane naturelle, tapia, mangrove ou terrain agricole déjà cultivé.
  2. Protéger l’existant : privilégier la régénération naturelle et les espèces indigènes d’origine locale dans les milieux naturels.
  3. Pour les champs : demander au SOC et au FOFIFA des variétés homologuées adaptées au district (par exemple sorgho, niébé, arachide). Tester d’abord sur une petite parcelle.
  4. Pour les arbres : contacter le SNGF à Ambatobe (+261 34 39 818 05) ; vérifier espèce, provenance, disponibilité et autorisations.
  5. Suivre les résultats : noter les semis, les plants vivants, les récoltes, l’eau utilisée, les effets sur les espèces locales et les coûts.

Précaution : n’importez pas de graines ni de plantes vivantes sans les autorisations et certificats phytosanitaires requis. Ne transformez pas les zones humides et prairies naturelles en plantations d’arbres. Aucun achat, prix ou stock n’est garanti par cet outil.

First rule · Do no ecological harm

Introduction ≠ restoration

Madagascar has extreme endemism. “Bring a helpful tree from another country” is not a safe default. The atlas distinguishes restoring locally native species from using already cultivated crops on existing farms. New alien tree introductions have no automatic recommendation.

STOP / REVIEW: Before planting outdoors, confirm land rights and community consent, verify whether the site was historically forest, grassland, wetland or mangrove, establish taxonomic identity and local provenance, screen invasion risk, and check permits. Near protected habitat, obtain specialist sign-off. Importing live plants or seed requires applicable phytosanitary and import documentation. See the red flags.

✓ Native habitat recovery

Protect existing vegetation, repair fire/grazing or hydrological stresses, and favor assisted natural regeneration. Add locally sourced seedlings only when needed.

Supported by Kew and MBG [S2] [S12]

✓ Established food crops

For private or community farm plots, choose regionally approved sorghum, cowpea, peanuts, beans and other crops with seed quality and farmer preferences verified.

SOC, FOFIFA and Ministry [S6] [S8] [S9]

✕ Unreviewed aliens

Do not distribute foreign ornamental grasses, eucalyptus, Prosopis, Leucaena, Opuntia or invasive aquatic plants as a general restoration package.

Invasion literature and local Melaleuca example [S20] [S21]

Best approach: Forest protection; local native regeneration; agroforestry only on existing farms.

03 · Verified institutions, not speculative stores

Where to source locally

Procurement links lead to existing Malagasy institutions and registries. This guide does not claim live stock, price, shipping, particular seed-lot certification or the right to collect wild plants. Call and confirm before visiting or paying.

🌳 SNGF — forest seeds & native plants

Public national forest seed institution. Ambatobe, BP 5091, Antananarivo 101. Ministry-listed phone: +261 34 39 818 05. An October 15, 2026 ministry notice advertises planting-season seedlings including native species.

SNGF official details ↗

Ask for species, actual stock, wild/legal source, collecting area, seed lot, viability and transport authorization. [S4] [S5]

🌾 SOC — approved crop seed producers

The Malagasy official seed control service publishes approved establishments, crop species, certified varieties and regional registers. Match the variety to district and season, not just crop name.

Browse accredited sellers ↗

Enquire about germination test, label, variety, harvest year and plant health. [S6] [S32]

🔬 FOFIFA — research & local varieties

FOFIFA provides seed programmes, research stations and regional contacts for rice, maize, beans and other crops. Refer to the station serving your agroecological zone.

FOFIFA seed programme ↗

FOFIFA is a research and seed-system contact, not a guarantee of retail availability. [S8]

What to ask before purchasing any seed or seedling
  1. What is the complete scientific species name and variety? Is it native to this particular district/site?
  2. What is the original parent-tree location or crop seed lot and certification number?
  3. When was it collected/harvested, and what is the tested germination or nursery survival rate?
  4. What pests/diseases are screened, and are quarantine/transport documents supplied?
  5. What habitat, rainfall, altitude, soil and water budget is recommended?
  6. Who owns the crop/forest and who benefits? Is there agreement for monitoring and benefit-sharing?
  7. Does the quote cover hardening, replacement failures, training, tools and aftercare?
If someone proposes shipping seeds from outside Madagascar

Do not order or send unsolicited seeds or live plants. Malagasy customs lists import authorization and a phytosanitary certificate for seed under heading 12.09, and phytosanitary controls for plant materials. Consult the competent Plant Protection Directorate (DPV) about species-specific quarantine and approvals first; conservation permits and biodiversity access conditions may be separate. [S10] [S11]

Prefer suitable, already-approved crops multiplied locally, or indigenous nursery supply. A species that grows in Australia/Africa/Latin America is not automatically safe in Madagascar.

04 · Establish only after site approval

Planting and care sequences

Native forest plot · 0 to 12 months

  1. Map vegetation history, land access and what native seedlings already regenerate.
  2. Address immediate threats: fire, uncontrolled browsing, invasive weeds, topsoil loss.
  3. Plan mixed locally occurring species with a botanical partner.
  4. Procure permitted seed lots; raise and harden seedlings in a local nursery.
  5. Plant into moist soil around onset of reliable local rains; keep root collar at soil grade.
  6. Mulch without touching stem where appropriate; protect seedlings from grazing.
  7. Check at weeks 2 and 6, months 3 and 6 and at 12 months; compare assisted regeneration against planting.

Site-specific methods vary. [S2] [S12] [S22]

Food crop plot · 0 to harvest

  1. Ask farmers which crops they want, which varieties they already know, and what seed they can access.
  2. Check soil fertility, salinity, slope, water, livestock pressure, existing tenure and market access.
  3. Run a small comparison: local control variety vs approved drought-tolerant option; do not gamble the whole harvest.
  4. Sow using specific packet/local FOFIFA advice; use cowpea/sorghum tested local varieties when appropriate.
  5. Record planting rain, emergence, pest burden, labor, harvest and food retained at home.
  6. Save certified or quality-controlled seed through approved channels for the next cycle.

Do not promise crops can grow without rain or irrigation. [S7] [S9] [S14]

Timing, cyclones, water and seedling diagnostics

Rain: Much of Madagascar has warmer wetter months roughly November–April, but regional timing varies and the east can be humid year-round. Do not sow on a calendar alone; require a verified field moisture window and short-range forecast. Cyclones: Stake seedlings as locally appropriate; protect nursery stock and avoid badly exposed planting days. Drought: prioritize water for people and existing food crops, not ornamental tree counts.

Diagnostic checks: wilted leaf with wet soil may indicate waterlogging; pale foliage may reflect nutrient or root disease; stem lesions in vanilla warrant sanitation; mangrove leaf stress may signal wrong tidal elevation; repeated field failure may mean wrong soil or rainfall rather than poor seed.

Practical nursery protocol and seed-lot traceability

Label every tray with taxon, origin, collection permission, collection date, germination batch and intended plot. Use clean nursery media and prevent cross-contamination. Do not guess scarification, soaking or cold-stratification for rare native species: ask SNGF or a botanist for validated species-specific protocols. Harden seedlings gradually under local shade and wind before transplanting. Reject root-bound or visibly diseased stock. Record seed-to-plant losses without hiding them.

05 · Outcomes, not planting-day photos

Monitoring that can falsify the intervention

Missouri Botanical Garden’s Madagascar protocol records numbers planted by species and restoration unit, and monitors at least 20 randomly selected and tagged trees per species/unit (or all if fewer), measuring height at planting and at 12 months. This atlas adds intermediate site checks; they are suggestions, not claimed MBG requirements. [S12]

🌱 0–12 months

Planted count, tagged sample, alive count, growth, seedling provenance, maintenance cost, browsing, disease and fire exposure.

🌾 1–3 growing seasons

Yield by variety, household dietary use, seed retention, soil cover, water inputs, farmer labor, failed crops and control plots.

🌿 1–5 years

Native recruitment, canopy and understory composition, invasive species, habitat connectivity, wetland hydrology and local ownership.

Interpret honestly: Seedlings surviving after 12 months ≠ forest restored. Crop yield improving ≠ food security achieved. A planted mangrove surviving ≠ a functioning estuary. Report negative results, uncertainty and counterfactual comparison alongside successes.

08 · Zero-harm / anti-inversion

Risk register & stop conditions

Ecological red flags

  • Alien species escapes managed fields or spreads into native vegetation.
  • Natural grassland, tapia woodland, marsh, seagrass or tidal flat is treated as “empty land.”
  • Unverified native range or genetic provenance; invasive weeds hitchhike in nursery soil.
  • Firewood or groundwater demand increases faster than habitat recovers.
  • Single species nursery production replaces a functioning natural ecosystem.

Human & institutional red flags

  • Planting displaces grazers, subsistence plots or customary community rights.
  • Protective fences deny access to legitimate water routes or forage.
  • Children work in hazardous tree planting, seed processing or pesticide handling.
  • School food gardens reduce nutrition by diverting scarce water from drinking supply.
  • Rare plants are publicized with precise harvestable coordinates or sold without permits.
Response: Pause the intervention, protect residents and existing habitats, document evidence without publicizing sensitive species locations, seek local ecological/community review, revise or remove the problematic component, and publish the correction. Any growth metric must be subordinate to food, water, rights and biodiversity.

09 · Integrate with resilience systems

Link vegetation to school meals and water stewardship

  • Household seed access

    Choose SOC/FOFIFA-approved, culturally preferred crop varieties. Trigger seed access using the forecast and field moisture, not an arbitrary monthly planting target.

  • School food ecosystems

    Put food crops in school-owned or authorized kitchen gardens only where water, labor, child protection, nutrition and long-term maintenance are secured.

  • Watershed protection

    Protect upstream native vegetation and establish infiltration/erosion observations before planting exotic fast-growing trees or building water-hungry nurseries.

  • District nursery network

    Source native flora through SNGF/community partners, crops through approved channels; maintain provenance labels and shared quality audit.

  • Farmer comparison trials

    Run farmer-chosen paired test fields with controls, transparent loss records and consent to publish only aggregated outcomes.

  • Public evidence receipts

    Aggregate survival, harvest, water and ecological indicators; never disclose sensitive locations or personal household information. Hash exported datasets for tamper detection, and digitally sign if proof of authorship matters.

    • A 90-day starter pilot for a drought-stressed district

    Days 1–15: consult farmer, school, district agriculture and environment representatives. Classify existing farmland versus natural land. Establish water and food needs, land consent and indigenous biodiversity checks.

    Days 16–30: check SOC varieties, select 2–3 farmer-approved crops; choose a small field comparison and safe storage. Review locally native vegetation already regenerating nearby.

    Days 31–60: establish only after rainfall/soil-moisture conditions are adequate; tag plants, measure baseline, train volunteer monitors and keep planting areas out of native habitats.

    Days 61–90: survival/pest/yield review, food distribution and water-demand audit, farmer feedback, and public aggregate evidence release with safeguards. Hold or redesign any intervention failing biodiversity or human safeguards.
